Project Code Dataset Website Code

Sensory-robust multimodal vla an study on how to make robust and multimodal VLAs.

Abstract

State-of-the-art Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models rely mostly on RGB perception and suffer catastrophic performance degradation under sensor failure

We propose, study, and discard a general pipeline and training strategy that is focused on mitigating this dependence and allowing for massive multi-modality out-of-the-box. The main building block of our pipeline is the 4M-21 model, capable of combining the inputs into a latent representation. This abstraction is then passed through a MLP to a fine-tuned SmolVLA that handles action generation, potentially leading to a multi-modal robust VLA capable of handling sensor corruption and even full modality dropout.

We show that this approach is not sufficient for the task and analyze the factors that make it fail. After showing the characteristics this approach is lacking, we propose multiple solutions to improve the pipeline and achieve multi-modal robustness.

I. Introduction

Current VLA models are not designed with sensor robustness in mind: they assume all inputs are clean and present at inference time. Yet recent work shows that even small perturbations such as Gaussian noise, motion blur, or spatter can cause success rates to drop from 90% to below 10% [4]. In real deployments, sensors fail, get occluded or degrade and a robot policy that fails under these conditions is not deployable. That is why we aim to build a VLA that is more robust to sensor errors.

Why does this matter?

  • Deployment safety. Real robots face hardware failures. The camera used by a robotic arm can get cracked or scratched, and the sensors in a mobile robot can get dirty or occluded during task execution. A policy that degrades gracefully rather than catastrophically is a prerequisite for reliable deployment.
  • Sensor diversity. Integrating depth or semantic features risks a harmful dependency where sensor failure yields worse performance than an RGB-only baseline. This work strives to safely unlock multi-sensor capabilities.
  • Research gap. Current state-of-the-art robustness techniques, such as RobustVLA [4], focus on visual noise and RGB perturbations. No existing work studies what happens when a sensor is entirely absent at inference time across richer multimodal settings.

Our approach. We test a sensor-robust multi-modal VLA that (i) leverages four complementary modalities (RGB, depth, segmentation, thermal) and (ii) is trained with corruption, using a frozen 4M-21 encoder and a modality-dropout curriculum. (iii) We then propose changes and upgrades to the pipeline that could lead to better results and highlight the shortcomings of this approach.

II. Related Works

Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models have become the dominant paradigm for general-purpose robot manipulation. SmolVLA [1], our base model, is a lightweight VLA that pairs a frozen SmolVLM2 vision-language backbone with a flow-matching action expert, matching much larger models. The larger π0 [2] established the flow-matching design SmolVLA inherits. Both, however, assume a fixed RGB sensor suite that is clean and fully present at inference time — the assumption our work targets.

Robustness of VLAs. Recent analyses show this assumption is fragile. LIBEROPlus [3] and Guo et al. [4] report that mild corruptions (noise, blur, partial occlusion) can collapse success rates from ~90% to below 10%. RobustVLA tackles this through robustness-aware post-training but focuses on RGB noise and does not consider full sensor disappearance. CRT [5] reconstructs corrupted RGB inputs before the policy. All these methods improve resilience within the RGB modality; none addresses the case where an entire modality is absent at test time, which is our setting.

Multimodal VLAs. OmniVLA [6] adds infrared, mmWave and acoustic sensing via masked-image overlays. ForceVLA [7] adds force sensing to π0 through a mixture-of-experts module, motivating our plug-in adapter design. Both require the new sensors to be present at test time and do not analyze partial sensor failure. Our perception backbone is 4M-21 [8], which fuses arbitrary subsets of available modalities, with ImageBind [9] bridging in synthetic thermal from ThermalGen [14]. We adopt the MLP connector design of LLaVA-1.5 [10], and a curriculum dropout schedule inspired by masked-training strategies for sensor-robust control [11, 12]. Evaluation uses LIBERO [13] with our corruption protocol.

III. Methodology

Our multimodal pipeline. Block 1 (perception): four modalities (RGB, depth, segmentation, thermal) pass through a modality-dropout layer; the frozen 4M-21 encoder fuses the available modalities into a unified token sequence. Block 2 (action): tokens are projected into SmolLM2's space via an MLP connector (redpath), replacing the SigLIP route of vanilla SmolVLA, then drive the flow-matching action expert.

3.1 Architecture

Our pipeline replaces SmolVLA's SigLIP encoder with a frozen 4M-21 backbone and bridges it back into the policy with a small trainable connector.

Perception. 4M-21 is the central design choice: because it is trained to operate on arbitrary subsets of its input modalities, dropping a sensor at inference is in-distribution for it rather than a degenerate input. The frozen 4M-21 XL encoder fuses the available modalities into a token sequence (B, 196, D). Thermal is embedded beforehand by the frozen ImageBind encoder into a 1024-D vector that 4M-21 accepts natively.

Action. A two-layer GELU MLP connector (LLaVA-1.5 style [10]) projects the 4M-21 tokens into SmolLM2's embedding space. These are concatenated with language and robot-state tokens and passed to the SmolVLA policy, whose flow-matching action expert outputs continuous 7-DoF action chunks. Only the connector and LoRA adapters are trainable (~8M parameters), against ~2B frozen across 4M-21, ImageBind and SmolVLA, keeping training feasible on one Tesla V100 GPU.

3.2 Dataset

We build on binhng/libero_10_lerobot_mask_depth, a LeRobot-formatted version of LIBERO-10 providing aligned RGB, depth, and segmentation. Since LIBERO has no thermal sensor, we augment it with a synthetic thermal modality generated from RGB via ThermalGen [14]. Each Parquet shard is processed by generating thermal frames and appending them as an aligned modality; with batch inference across parallel SLURM jobs we reduce preprocessing from ~100h to ~13h. ThermalGen is synthetic and a known weak link — its outputs are not real thermal measurements, a caveat we carry into the analysis.

3.3 Modality-Dropout Curriculum and Training

Robustness is induced by the training schedule. During training each modality is independently dropped or corrupted with probability 0.5, following a curriculum that transitions from clean inputs to full dropout: corruption starts mild and grows in severity over epochs, drawn from Gaussian noise, horizontal motion blur and/or centered occlusion. The model thus first learns multimodal fusion under near-clean conditions, then progressively learns to rely on redundant cues as modalities degrade or disappear. This is what prevents the harmful-dependency failure mode: by repeatedly removing each sensor, none becomes indispensable.

Training proceeds in two stages: Stage 1 trains only the MLP connector with all pretrained modules frozen, aligning 4M-21 features with SmolLM2's token distribution; Stage 2 fine-tunes the connector and LoRA adapters under the full dropout curriculum. We validate incrementally to isolate failures: first an RGB-only run with no corruption, to verify the pipeline matches the vanilla SmolVLA baseline and the connector is not the bottleneck; then the dropout curriculum on RGB alone; and only once both pass do we add depth, segmentation, and thermal, evaluating under clean inputs, single-modality dropout, and the corruption protocol. This staged design lets us attribute any regression to a specific addition — a modality, the curriculum or the connector.

IV. Experiments

4.1 Implementation details

All experiments are conducted on the EPFL IZAR cluster using NVIDIA V100 GPUs. The pretrained 4M-21 encoder and SmolVLA backbone remain frozen throughout training. Only a lightweight MLP projector is optimized to map 4M visual tokens into the latent space expected by SmolVLA

4.2 Analysis of Encoder Replacement

Our first approach consisted of directly replacing the original SigLIP visual encoder of SmolVLA with the 4M-21 encoder while training only a MLP connector using the downstream action loss. All experiments in this section were conducted using clean RGB observations in order to isolate the effect of the encoder replacement itself and to validate the pipeline.

The underlying assumption was that policy supervision alone would implicitly align the projected 4M representations with the latent space expected by the pretrained SmolVLA backbone.

However, this approach completely failed during evaluation, reaching a 0% success rate on LIBERO tasks. The evaluated tasks included manipulation sequences such as:

  • Putting both the alphabet soup and the tomato sauce into the basket
  • Turning on the stove and placing the moka pot on it
  • Placing different mugs onto designated plates

To better understand the source of failure, we progressively analyzed the system in a top-down fashion. We first verified that the original SmolVLA pipeline reproduced the expected LIBERO performance, reaching approximately 80% success rate. We then focused specifically on the modified component: the encoder replacement pipeline composed of the 4M-21 encoder and the MLP projector.

Global Representation Misalignment

We first investigated the global organization of the latent spaces using t-SNE visualization. The figure below shows the latent representations produced by SigLIP and by the projected 4M + MLP features before introducing any distillation objective.

Initially, the two latent spaces were strongly separated, indicating severe incompatibility between the projected 4M embeddings and the representations expected by SmolVLA. To quantify this mismatch, we computed both the mean cosine similarity and the mean token norm between SigLIP and projected 4M features.

These results confirmed that the projected 4M embeddings were both geometrically misaligned and strongly mismatched in magnitude compared to SigLIP representations. To address this issue, we introduced a global cosine alignment loss between the projected 4M + MLP embeddings and the original SigLIP features. The objective operated on mean-pooled visual embeddings, encouraging the global latent representations to become closer in direction.

After distillation, the projected 4M + MLP embeddings became significantly closer to the SigLIP manifold and the cosine similarity improved substantially.

Although the latent spaces appeared globally closer, the robotic policy still failed completely during evaluation and continued producing unstable behaviors, including repetitive motions and gripper collapse, where the robot converged toward degenerate open-gripper trajectories. This result suggested that global latent alignment alone was insufficient. More specifically, our initial distillation objective operated on mean-pooled embeddings, where all spatial tokens were averaged before computing the cosine loss. While this improved global latent geometry, it did not constrain the alignment of individual visual tokens.

However, SmolVLA relies heavily on transformer cross-attention between language tokens and spatial visual tokens. Consequently, preserving token-level correspondence appeared critical for maintaining downstream robotic behavior. To further investigate this hypothesis, we replaced the global mean-pooled distillation objective with a token-level cosine alignment loss operating directly on each visual token independently. This modification explicitly enforced local spatial alignment rather than only global latent similarity.

Token-level distillation significantly improved representation alignment, reaching a mean cosine similarity of approximately 0.77. However, we simultaneously observed large instability in token magnitudes, motivating the introduction of LayerNorm inside the MLP projector to stabilize the projected representations.

To verify whether token-level distillation successfully preserved spatial correspondence, we computed pairwise cosine similarity between all SigLIP and projected 4M visual tokens. After token-level distillation, the similarity matrix exhibited a strong diagonal structure, indicating that corresponding spatial regions became properly aligned between both latent spaces. This result confirmed that token-level supervision successfully restored local spatial consistency, unlike the previous mean-pooled objective which only aligned global representations.

Despite the strong geometric and spatial alignment obtained after token-level distillation, the robotic policy still failed during downstream evaluation.

Table 3 reveals a major scale mismatch between SigLIP and the projected 4M representations. While SigLIP produces a mean L2 norm of 36.05, the 4M+MLP tokens have a mean norm of 16.0 due to the LayerNorm applied inside the MLP connector. This difference is critical because the projected visual tokens are directly injected into the pretrained transformer backbone of SmolVLA. Inside SmolVLA, visual and language tokens interact through transformer attention layers based on dot-product operations between embeddings. Although the cosine distillation loss successfully aligned token directions, it did not constrain their magnitudes. As a result, the projected 4M embeddings remained significantly smaller than the original SigLIP representations used during SmolVLA pretraining. This scale mismatch likely weakened the contribution of visual tokens inside the transformer backbone, leading to degraded spatial reasoning and unstable robotic behavior.

Overall, our experiments revealed that encoder replacement inside pretrained VLAs is substantially more difficult than simple latent alignment. Even after recovering strong cosine similarity, spatial token correspondence, and compatible latent geometry, the robotic policy still failed during downstream evaluation. Our results suggest that successful encoder replacement requires preserving multiple properties simultaneously:

  • Latent geometry
  • Spatial token structure
  • The activation scales expected by the pretrained transformer

V. Conclusion

The primary objective of this project was to construct and evaluate a multimodal Vision-Language-Action architecture capable of mapping sensory inputs to continuous robotic control trajectories. To this end, we engineered an end-to-end training and evaluation pipeline that integrated a multimodal vision encoder with a language model backbone, ultimately conditioning a flow-matching action expert. However, empirical evaluation of the final trained model resulted in a 0% task success rate. The model consistently failed to execute targeted manipulation tasks, exhibiting this failure even when evaluated on baseline, uncorrupted data without the planned introduction of sensor dropouts.

Diagnostic analysis indicated that the action expert experienced severe mode collapse. Rather than generating context-aware, dynamic movement trajectories, the network consistently converged on a static output corresponding to the statistical average of the training dataset. These results suggest that the flow-matching action module is highly sensitive to inconsistencies in the conditioning representations. Mapping a vision backbone to a language model using a standard MLP connector may be insufficient for this task. While the project successfully established the complex software infrastructure required to fuse these disparate architectural components, the failure to achieve functional robotic control underscores the significant difficulty of ensuring representation alignment in composite, multimodal systems.

VI. Limitations and Future Work

The defining limitation of our current implementation involves the highly unstable training dynamics and the resulting fragility of the learned representations. The collapse of the action head appears to be associated with instability in the representations produced by the vision encoder and connector. The simple MLP connector utilized to bridge the high-dimensional multimodal latent space and the language model's embedding space likely lacked the structural capacity to resolve such complex distributions. Because the action expert conditioned its outputs on these fluctuating representations, it is hypothesized that the flow-matching loss could not find a stable gradient, ultimately defaulting to a safe-mode average.

This methodological limitation was heavily exacerbated by severe computational bottlenecks during the evaluation phase. Our current testing framework proved computationally demanding, requiring approximately 12 hours to evaluate a standard suite of 100 robotic episodes (roughly seven minutes per episode). This constrained our ability to conduct rigorous hyperparameter sweeps, isolate the exact steps where representation degradation occurred, or rapidly prototype alternative architectural configurations.

Several directions could be explored in future work:

  • Evaluating alternative vision encoders to determine if differing pretraining objectives extract inherently more stable initial features for robotic control.
  • Investigating more expressive connector architectures to accurately project high-variance multimodal signals into the language model without critical information loss.
  • Developing alignment objectives that better preserve information across modalities, ensuring visual tokens are demonstrably stabilized before any fine-tuning is attempted on the action expert.

Although the final policy did not achieve successful task execution, the project highlighted several practical challenges involved in adapting pretrained vision-language architectures for robotic control. In particular, the experiments underscored the importance of stable representation alignment between perception and action modules. These observations can help guide future investigations into multimodal systems and provide a clearer understanding of the factors that may limit end-to-end performance.
